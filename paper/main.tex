\section*{Abstract}

Solvent response governs hydration free energy, yet high-fidelity calculations
resolve it afresh for every new molecule. We ask whether information already
encoded in disparate solvent datasets can instead be learned once and reused.
SolvAI trains molecular surrogates on six calculated and empirical sources,
predicts 15 complementary response descriptors from a SMILES string, and combines
them with structural features in a separate predictor fitted to experimental
hydration free energies. Inference requires no new solvent simulation. In a
matched ExtraTrees comparison, the descriptors reduce mean absolute error from
0.303 to 0.202 kcal mol$^{-1}$ on 85 ARROW solutes and from 1.532 to 1.153
kcal mol$^{-1}$ on an endpoint-disjoint external cohort of 220 molecules.
Shuffling descriptors among molecules removes the gain; it persists on the
97-molecule subset also absent from supervised response sources. The same
descriptors often help other endpoint families, although the best complete
predictor and the most useful source mixture change with the evaluation
chemistry. Existing solvent calculations can therefore provide reusable
supervision for molecular prediction, while source choice and transfer beyond
small neutral solutes remain important tests.

\section*{Introduction}

Hydration free energy is the reversible work of transferring a molecule from the gas
phase into water. It connects molecular structure to partitioning, binding and
reactivity, and is a stringent test of how a model balances electrostatics,
polarization, dispersion, repulsion and solvent reorganization. Experimental values
are available for many small neutral compounds, notably through FreeSolv, but
predicting them with comparable precision remains difficult.\cite{freesolv2014,freesolv2017}

Physical simulation resolves solvent response directly. Alchemical methods vary
the solute--water interaction along a coupling path and combine the resulting
ensemble averages; path-integral molecular dynamics (PIMD) can also represent
nuclear delocalization.\cite{mbar2008,nqereview2018} The cost is that each new
molecule calls for another sampling calculation. On 85 neutral solutes, the ARROW
study made the accuracy at stake concrete: classical ARROW has a reconstructed
mean absolute error (MAE) of \ClassicalMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$, falling to
\PIMDMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ with eight-bead PIMD (PIMD8).\cite{arrow2022}
Learned-potential extensions reproduce parts of that physical description, but
still use trajectories at inference.\cite{arrownn2023,nqe2024}

Prediction from molecular structure offers a different economy: train on earlier
examples, then answer a new query without repeating their calculations. Graph and
three-dimensional predictors, $\Delta$-learning and multi-fidelity methods use
this principle in different ways.\cite{ramakrishnan2015,combisolv2021,rauer2020,ward2021}
Physically inspired descriptors can bring electrostatics, accessible surface or
conformational response into such models.\cite{mldescriptors2023,fml2021}
Yet approaches that calculate a continuum or molecular solvent response for each
query retain a per-molecule physical step.\cite{hybridfepml2020,mlpcm2021,rism2020,imperham2022}
Can that solvent information itself become a reusable molecular representation?

Predicting a physical descriptor before fitting a downstream property is an
established route: quantum-chemical descriptors have been learned for reactivity,
and a two-stage hydration model has predicted solute descriptors instead of
calculating them for every query.\cite{mlqmgnn2022,twostagehfe2026}
The usefulness of such a descriptor depends on its relevance, how accurately it
can be predicted and how the downstream model uses it.\cite{qmhelp2024,surrogatelatent2025}
Here the question is whether several complementary kinds of solvent information,
learned from different sources, remain useful across hydration predictors and
unfamiliar molecules.

SolvAI uses the existing datasets as six groups of teachers (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}).
They supply 15 targets spanning calculated transfer free energies, empirical
solute properties and summaries of how water changes conformer energies. Molecular
surrogates learn to predict these quantities from structure and are frozen before
a separate endpoint learns experimental hydration free energy. We call each
predicted quantity a \emph{response descriptor}; the name does not imply a
derivative with respect to an applied field. Because the two learning stages are
separate, we can change the hydration predictor while holding the descriptors
fixed, or change their surrogates while holding the source targets fixed.

We first ask whether the predicted responses improve a matched hydration predictor
and whether the gain survives controls that break molecule alignment or separate
training from test chemistry. We then hold the response representation fixed and
vary the endpoint, distinguishing the value of adding responses to a given learner
from the accuracy of the complete learner. Finally, we vary the information used
to build the representation and test its behavior on larger molecules. The later
architecture comparisons are retrospective; their selection protocol is detailed
in Methods.

\section*{SolvAI: learning solvent response before hydration free energy}

\subsection*{Two learning tasks with different supervision}

For a neutral molecule represented by a SMILES string $x$, the target is its
experimental gas-to-water transfer free energy $y=\Delta G_{\rm hyd}$ near 298 K.
We first train separate molecular models on external source datasets. Each model
predicts one or more source quantities, such as a continuum-solvent free energy or
a hydrogen-bonding parameter. These models are \emph{surrogates}: after training,
they approximate the source quantities without repeating the source calculation or
measurement. Their weights are frozen before learning the experimental hydration
predictor.

Writing the frozen surrogate predictions as $\widehat r_j(x)$, our model is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\boldsymbol r}(x)=(\widehat r_1(x),\ldots,\widehat r_{15}(x)),
 \qquad \widehat y(x)=h\!\left([\boldsymbol s(x),\widehat{\boldsymbol r}(x)]\right),
 \label{eq:solvai}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol s(x)$ denotes structural features; the standard tabular input
contains a 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprint and 217 RDKit two-dimensional descriptors.
The endpoint $h$ learns how the predicted source quantities relate to experiment;
it does not sum them as if they were components of one physical free energy.
The primary comparisons keep the same 15 frozen predictions while varying $h$
and, in some models, the structural representation (Table~\ref{tab:endpoints}).
All 15 quantities are predicted at deployment, including those whose original
source targets were experimental.

\subsection*{What the response descriptors represent}

The sources offer different views of solvation rather than successive rungs on
one fidelity scale (Table~\ref{tab:sources}; Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}b).
Continuum-solvent calculations estimate transfer energetics; Abraham parameters
describe empirical interaction tendencies; corrected explicit- and implicit-solvent
estimates combine a physical prediction with an experimental residual. ConfSolv
adds information about how water redistributes conformer energies and solvation
responses. Together they give the endpoint several kinds of evidence about a
molecule, rather than asking one source to stand for the whole solvent problem.
The source methods and their physical definitions are documented in the original
literature and Supplementary Methods~S3 and Table~1.\cite{cosmo1995,combisolv2021,soluteml2022,openffsage2023,gbn22013,smd2009,confsolv2023}

The source archives contain many calculations per molecule. MolSolv's
1,729,545 records are SMD(water) calculations; ConfSolv's 5,392,567 are
water-conformer records. After quality, target and identity filtering, the
respective surrogate tables retain \MolSolvN{} and \ConfSolvN{} molecular
connectivities. Archive records and training rows are different
units of counting (Supplementary Table~1 and Fig.~2).\cite{molsolv2022}

Hydration free energy is an equilibrium property of a molecule, not a separate
experimental label for each constrained geometry. ConfSolv's six coordinates
summarize conformational response without reconstructing an ensemble at
inference. Supplementary Fig.~7 illustrates this distinction, which also matters
when comparing the response features with Uni-Mol's generated three-dimensional
conformer.\cite{unimol2023}

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Separating source supervision from hydration evaluation}

Two experimental settings ask different questions. ARROW-85 provides experimental
hydration values and a published high-fidelity simulation result for the same
small molecules; its folds test the learned predictor out of sample. A separate
cohort of 220 neutral solutes, drawn from original Henry-law measurements, tests
prediction outside the endpoint training labels. All comparisons of SolvAI in
this paper use experiment as the reference; the ARROW simulation is an accuracy
context, not a model input.

The endpoint training pool contains 1,280 public hydration labels plus available
ARROW training folds. The response-source tables exclude every exact or standardized
equivalent of the 85 ARROW test solutes. All 220 external molecules are absent from
endpoint labels, and a nested set of 97 is absent from the supervised response
sources as well. We distinguish these two forms of exposure throughout because a
source calculation for a molecule is not its experimental hydration label.
Exclusions and affected surrogate refits are documented in Methods, Supplementary
Table~2 and Supplementary Data~4.

\subsection*{A matched test of reusable response information}



We begin with a matched question: do the predicted responses add information
when the hydration learner stays the same? An ExtraTrees ensemble of randomized
decision trees\cite{extratrees2006} is trained with or without the 15 response
columns, using identical labels, folds, structural features and training recipe.
Each ARROW solute is predicted out of fold by a fit that did not receive its
experimental label. Structural features alone yield an MAE of \MatchedMAE{}
kcal mol$^{-1}$; the first eight response descriptors reduce it to \NarrowMAE{},
SMD(water) to \NarrowSMDMAE{}, and the six conformer summaries to
\SolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}b). That final value is
numerically close to the published ARROW/PIMD8 error of \PIMDMAE{} on these
solutes. PIMD8 is a separate physical simulation, while this endpoint learns
from experimental hydration labels; the matched test here is the comparison
with and without response descriptors.

The gain is distributed across molecules rather than driven by a single large
correction: absolute error falls for 53 of 85 solutes, and the paired MAE change
is $-\SolvAIReduction{}$ kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% bootstrap interval,
\SolvAICILow{} to \SolvAICIHigh{}; Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}c). The full set also
improves more than the tested computed-energy, SMD-only or ConfSolv-only blocks in
isolation (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}d). The empirical and residual-corrected block
contributes on its own, suggesting that the sources supply partly complementary
information. Molecule-level predictions and residuals are in Supplementary
Fig.~1 and Data~2.

Shuffling tests whether the benefit depends on assigning the responses to the correct
molecule (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}a). Across five independent
permutations of the 15-column block, the fixed-partition mean is \ShuffledMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$,
close to the 0.303 matched control and far from the aligned-response fit. The paired difference
between aligned and mean-shuffled descriptors is $-0.104$ kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% interval,
$-0.214$ to $-0.025$). Alignment between a molecule and its predicted response is
therefore necessary for the observed gain.



Changing the ARROW partition changes the absolute estimate but preserves the
matched contrast. Across five complete five-fold partitions, the response-augmented
endpoint has an average MAE of \RepeatMean{} $\pm$ \RepeatSD{} kcal mol$^{-1}$,
versus \MatchedRepeatMean{} for structure alone, and improves in every partition
(Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}e; Supplementary Table~4). Reducing the preset weight of
available ARROW training labels from 3 to 1 also preserves the contrast
(Supplementary Table~9).

\subsection*{Transfer beyond nearby labels}

Random molecular folds can place close chemical neighbors on opposite sides of a
train--test split. We therefore repeated the ExtraTrees comparison after excluding
endpoint-training molecules in the test family's scaffold, functional family or
molecular cluster, and after removing neighbors above fixed Morgan-similarity
thresholds. Each rule applies to the entire endpoint-supervised pool; the response
surrogates remain fixed.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
Adding responses lowers MAE under every predeclared separation rule
(Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}a,b; Supplementary Table~5). At a similarity exclusion
threshold of 0.70, the matched error falls from \NNSeventyMatchedMAE{} to
\NNSeventySolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$. Scaffold and functional-family splits are
harder in absolute terms, yet their paired intervals also favor the response
features. The gain therefore persists without nearby endpoint labels; it does
not make these difficult splits equally accurate. Full regime values and
molecule-level family errors are in Supplementary Tables~5--6 and Fig.~5.

Removing all ARROW experimental labels from endpoint training asks whether the
representation needs fold-local adaptation to those 85 solutes. Trained only on
the 1,280 public labels, the structure-only endpoint has an MAE of
\ZeroArrowMatchedMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$, versus \ZeroArrowMAE{} with responses
(paired interval for the change, $-0.246$ to $-0.037$; Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}c).
This is an ARROW-label removal test; the separate external cohort tests a more
distinct set of molecules.

The external cohort supplies the stronger transfer test. It was frozen from
original Henry-law measurements for a separate solvation-model study before
that study's predictions were examined.\cite{sander2023} On its \TierAN{} endpoint-disjoint neutral
solutes, responses lower matched MAE from \TierAStructureMAE{} to
\TierASolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ (paired change \TierADelta{}; 95\% interval,
\TierACILow{} to \TierACIHigh{}; Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}d).

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
Among the nested
\TierAStrictN{} solutes absent from all six supervised response sources, the
corresponding contrast is \TierAStrictStructureMAE{} to
\TierAStrictSolvAIMAE{} (paired change \TierAStrictDelta{}; interval,
\TierAStrictCILow{} to \TierAStrictCIHigh{}). The strict subset's median absolute
error moves in the opposite direction, from 1.102 to 1.244; the mean improvement
is therefore not a gain for every molecule or every error summary
(Supplementary Table~10). Absolute errors are also much larger here than on
ARROW-85.

The largest absolute gain occurs where external molecules are least like the
endpoint training pool (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}e). Among 35 solutes with nearest
training-set Morgan similarity below 0.30, MAE is 4.027 kcal mol$^{-1}$ from
structural features and 2.501 with responses. This is an encouraging reduction
in a difficult region, but the remaining error is still substantial. The full
similarity-bin counts and estimates are shown in the figure.

The response set mixes computed quantities with empirical parameters and experimental
residual corrections. To distinguish their roles, an additional control (Fig.~\ref{fig:composition}) retains ten
computed-source descriptors: COSMOtherm, uncorrected OpenFF and GBn2, SMD(water), and

remain unchanged. On ARROW-85 this control reaches \ComputedOnlyMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$;

(\ComputedOnlyLow{} to \ComputedOnlyHigh{} kcal mol$^{-1}$). The full 15-descriptor
model is better than this control by 0.032 kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% interval for full
minus computation-only, $-0.063$ to $-0.006$).
\par\endgroup

The ordering reverses outside the reference chemistry. The ten-descriptor model
reaches 1.095 kcal mol$^{-1}$ on the external 220 molecules, compared with 1.153 for
the full model. The paired full-minus-computation-only difference is $0.057$
kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% interval, $0.023$ to $0.093$). On the strict 97-molecule
subset, the corresponding errors are 1.422 and 1.536 kcal mol$^{-1}$, with a
paired interval of $0.058$ to $0.172$ for the difference. Computed-source descriptors
therefore reduce external error relative to structural features alone, while
the full source composition is more effective on ARROW. This comparison identifies
a dependence on source composition and evaluation chemistry, rather than a
single source set that is uniformly preferable. Both fixed configurations are
reported; external errors were not used to retune either model.

\subsection*{Changing source surrogates changes the transfer trade-off}

The source mixture is only one design choice. Another is how accurately each
source quantity can be inferred from structure, and whether improving that
prediction helps hydration. In a retrospective challenge we held the 15 target
definitions fixed and compared alternate learners for the four source families
that had used trees. Source-only validation retained the released recipes for
Abraham and ConfSolv but selected ridge-plus-neural-residual surrogates for
OpenFF and GBn2 (Supplementary Method~S22; Table~17). Their individual source
targets were predicted more accurately, whereas the corrected OpenFF and GBn2
descriptors passed downstream showed smaller, unresolved changes. Better
targetwise source scores did not automatically produce better hydration features.

Replacing only those two surrogate families made the trade-off visible in the
hydration endpoint. With a protocol-matched ExtraTrees refit, ARROW-85 MAE moves
from 0.203 to 0.238 kcal mol$^{-1}$, while External-220 improves from 1.192 to
1.075 and its source-disjoint Strict-97 subset from 1.595 to 1.352 (paired
Strict-97 interval for the change, $-0.358$ to $-0.137$; Supplementary
Table~18). A second endpoint has no clearly resolved gain, and the ExtraTrees
size-held-out error rises. These results show that source-learner choice can
favor one transfer setting without improving the others. The challenge does
not replace the released model; source exposure and the full selection protocol
are documented in Methods and Supplementary Method~S22.

\subsection*{When response prediction is insufficient}

Some plausible sources do not survive the two-stage route. In exploratory
development screens, compact ConfSolv summaries helped more than the tested
latent representations from the same source; additional force-field,
pair-interaction and implicit-solvent features did not improve their matched
endpoints (Supplementary Fig.~3). A useful source target must be relevant to
hydration, predictable from structure and complementary to the information
already supplied by other descriptors.

We also predicted coupling-path energy derivatives from short two-bead
path-integral simulations (PIMD2). Although the surrogates captured broad
trends, their component MAEs ranged from 1.27 to 5.20 kcal mol$^{-1}$.
Adding the predicted response increased its separate exploratory endpoint
error from 0.196 to 0.201 kcal mol$^{-1}$; no PIMD-derived coordinate enters
the final model. The fuller hierarchy and direct-integration tests are in
Supplementary Fig.~4 and Data~1. These outcomes test the learned descriptors
in this setting, rather than rank the physical source methods themselves.

\section*{Discussion}

The value of SolvAI lies in changing when solvent information is used. Instead
of resolving a new solvent environment for every query, it learns molecular
coordinates from earlier calculations and measurements, then lets an experimental
hydration endpoint decide how those coordinates matter. Predicted physical
descriptors and multi-fidelity transfer have precedents;\cite{mlqmgnn2022,ramakrishnan2015,combisolv2021,twostagehfe2026}
the test here is whether several heterogeneous solvent sources can be combined
and reused across new molecules and learners. The matched ExtraTrees contrast
shows a gain beyond structural features, and shuffling the response block among
molecules removes it. Chemical-separation and external-cohort tests show that
part of this information remains useful beyond nearby endpoint labels.

Two kinds of portability matter here. A response representation is reusable if
different hydration learners can extract value from it; a complete predictor
transfers only if it remains accurate in new chemistry. The first property is
supported by the paired gains across several endpoint families, including fits
with pretrained molecular encodings. It does not follow from ranking complete
models by error: the lowest-error learner changes across cohorts, and its own
response ablation can be neutral or unfavorable. Keeping these questions apart
shows what the frozen surrogates contribute without attributing every model
improvement to them.

The source-composition reversal shows why adding more teachers is not a
monotonic route to transfer. Empirical parameters and residual corrections help
the full mixture on ARROW-85, while the fixed computed-source subset has lower
error on both external cohorts (Fig.~\ref{fig:composition}). Likewise, a more
accurate surrogate for its own source target need not yield a better hydration
feature. These comparisons do not identify a single mechanism for the reversal;
the source distributions, prediction errors and endpoint use of each coordinate
can all matter. They do show that source selection must be evaluated on the
intended deployment chemistry, with independent labels held out from that choice.

The same distinction between promising behavior and verified accuracy governs
larger molecules. The labelled size split tests a different endpoint regime,
but earlier development had already seen its labels. Peptide and alkane curves
show how predictions behave as structures grow; without reference free energies,
they cannot validate that behavior. The failed PIMD2 descriptors and undercovered
predictive intervals further show that a physically motivated target or a
numerical uncertainty estimate is not automatically useful at deployment. The
present evidence is for neutral molecules near room temperature. It does not
establish performance for ions, other solvents, conformer-resolved energies or
forces.

Independent larger-molecule and new-solvent references are the next decisive
tests. They would allow source learners to be judged by downstream utility
as well as recovery of their own targets, and would show how far solvent
information learned once can replace a calculation repeated for every new
molecule.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Reference set and targets}

The primary evaluation uses the 85 neutral organic solutes reported in the ARROW
hydration analysis at 298 K.\cite{arrow2022} One water condition was retained per
connectivity. The released benchmark table contains canonical structure,
experimental hydration free energy, experimental uncertainty where available,
classical ARROW, four-bead PIMD (PIMD4) and PIMD8 values, and all fold assignments. The exact
reconstructed PIMD8 MAE is \PIMDMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$; the source article reports
approximately 0.20 kcal mol$^{-1}$.

\subsection*{Endpoint labels and features}

The public training pool contains 1,280 neutral, benchmark-disjoint experimental
hydration labels: 1,147 connectivity-resolved records from a FreeSolv/CombiSolv-Exp
merge and 133 SoluteML dGsolvDB3 records supported by at least two source
measurements.\cite{freesolv2017,combisolv2021,soluteml2022} The ExtraTrees input has a
2,048-bit radius-2 Morgan fingerprint and 217 RDKit two-dimensional descriptors.
Non-finite values are handled by a median imputer fitted inside each endpoint fit.

\subsection*{Response surrogates}

Fifteen response descriptors are generated by six surrogate families (Supplementary
Table~1). CheMeleon-initialized D-MPNNs predict two source families:
CombiSolv-QM/COSMOtherm and MolSolv SMD(water).\cite{chemprop2019,chemeleon2025}
ExtraTrees models predict five Abraham
axes and the physical and residual components of corrected OpenFF and GBn2 responses.
LightGBM models predict six ConfSolv conformer and response-distribution summaries.
The latter are derived from the H$_2$O subset of ConfSolv. The final coordinate list,
target units, source sizes, transformations and inference calculations are given in
Supplementary Table~1. PIMD-derived targets are absent from this list.

\subsection*{Retrospective source-surrogate architecture challenge}

We separately compared five fixed learner candidates for each of four
previously tree-based source families: the released tree recipe, an MLP using
binary Morgan bits, an MLP using count features, count-aware ridge and
count-aware ridge plus a bounded neural residual. The two D-MPNN source
families, all source target definitions and the endpoint labels remained
unchanged. Candidate choice used three source-development folds and a fixed
0.005 target-standardized-MAE tie margin favoring the simpler learner.
A 20\% source test, reserved before fitting, was opened only after choice was
locked. The source-development choice of Abraham's tree was retained despite
the lower test point estimate of an unselected hybrid. Full candidates, preprocessing,
targetwise and summed-source diagnostics appear in Supplementary Method~S22 and
Table~17.

Endpoint comparisons used three source conditions: the released response
matrix; a protocol-matched refit of the four tree families; and that refit with
only the preselected OpenFF and GBn2 hybrids substituted. Every exact endpoint
molecule appearing in a source table received source out-of-fold predictions;
none of the ARROW-85 or Strict-97 identities appeared in any supervised source
table. ExtraTrees and ridge-plus-neural-residual endpoints were refitted with
their own locked recipes, identical labels, folds and seeds across source
conditions. External-220 includes source-exposed molecules and is secondary to
Strict-97 for this source-stage challenge. Its paired intervals use 10,000
molecule resamples and are descriptive, not adjusted for model exploration.
The 205-molecule size refit and unlabelled alkane/peptide series are diagnostics,
not independent validation of extensivity (Supplementary Method~S22 and
Table~18).

\subsection*{Matched ExtraTrees endpoint}

For the ExtraTrees endpoint, an ensemble of three \texttt{ExtraTreesRegressor} pipelines\cite{extratrees2006} is used, each
with 360 trees, maximum feature fraction 0.7, minimum leaf size 2, no bootstrap and
random seeds 11, 29 and 47. Predictions are averaged. Public endpoint rows have
sample weight 1 and available ARROW outer-training rows weight 3. The matched
structure-only model is identical except that the 15 response columns are removed.

\subsection*{Identity standardization}

RDKit 2026.03.5 was used to generate canonical isomeric SMILES, full InChIKeys and
connectivity blocks.\cite{rdkit2026} The confirmatory audit additionally applied
MolStandardize cleanup, largest-fragment selection, uncharging and canonical tautomer
enumeration. Exact and standardized benchmark equivalents were removed from every
supervised external source. The audit found no such match in the endpoint pool. It
identified 2 CombiSolv-QM, 32 MolSolv and 22 ConfSolv source records beyond the
original exact-connectivity exclusion. The affected surrogates were refitted with all
remaining molecules held in their original train, validation or test split. Full pair
lists, identifiers and split-verification hashes are released.

\subsection*{Confirmatory evaluation}

Confirmatory analyses followed a prespecified protocol archived in Git. Documented
implementation amendments preceded the corrected runs (Supplementary Methods~S9). The
primary partition is the existing five-fold assignment on the 85 molecules. Five
complete shuffled K-fold partitions use seeds 314159, 271828, 161803, 141421 and
173205. In each outer fold, all 1,280 public labels and the available ARROW
outer-training labels are fitted; the test target is absent. Feature blocks,
ExtraTrees hyperparameters, sample weights and model seeds are identical across all
comparisons.

A separately prespecified one-factor sensitivity repeated the fixed folds after changing
only the available ARROW outer-training weight from 3 to 1. It retained all source
rows, surrogates, features, hyperparameters and seeds. Its paired interval used the same
100,000-resample convention.

For the negative control, the 15 response columns are permuted as one block, thereby
preserving their joint distribution while destroying molecule alignment. Public,
outer-training and outer-test response rows are permuted separately within each fold.
Five permutation seeds (88001--88005) are evaluated for the fixed partition and each
of the five repeats.

\subsection*{Chemical separation}

Separation is applied to the complete endpoint-supervised pool. Functional families
are assigned by a deterministic SMARTS precedence; five outer folds are formed by
group. Bemis--Murcko scaffolds are canonicalized, with acyclic structures grouped by
primary family. Taylor--Butina clusters use radius-2, 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprints
at Tanimoto similarity 0.70. For each outer test fold, every public or ARROW training
row sharing a held-out group is removed. A separate nearest-neighbor analysis removes
every training row with similarity at or above 0.50, 0.60, 0.70 or 0.80 to any test
molecule. No threshold was selected by performance. Exact SMARTS, assignments,
training counts and maximum retained similarities are released in Supplementary
Data~3 and the accompanying \nolinkurl{results/confirmatory/} tables.

\subsection*{Zero-ARROW transfer}

The frozen endpoint configuration was fitted once to the 1,280 public experimental
labels without any ARROW experimental value. Both matched structure-only and full
response-descriptor models then predicted all 85 ARROW molecules. This analysis measures
transfer without reference-set endpoint adaptation; it is not described as an
independent external test because model development used the reference chemistry.

\subsection*{External validation}

The candidate cohort was frozen for a separate solvation-model validation study,
before that study's model predictions, using original-measurement records from
Sander's Henry-law compilation v5.0.0.\cite{sander2023}
The intrinsic Henry solubility constant $H_s^{cp}$ is the aqueous concentration
divided by the gas partial pressure. Values at 298.15 K were converted using
$K_c=H_s^{cp}RT$ and $\Delta G_{\rm hyd}=-RT\ln K_c$, corresponding to 1 M ideal
gas and 1 M ideal-dilute aqueous standard states. Here $K_c$ is the dimensionless
aqueous-to-gas concentration ratio, $R$ the gas constant and $T$ the temperature.
Qualification preceded SolvAI
evaluation and required one connected neutral solute, supported elements, compatible
target metadata and no exact, fragment-parent, uncharged-parent or canonical-tautomer
equivalent in the 1,280 public or 85 ARROW endpoint labels. One previously
documented registry conflict was excluded, leaving 220 molecules. A strict
97-molecule subset additionally excludes any identity-equivalent occurrence in all
six supervised response-source tables.

For the matched ExtraTrees comparison, deployment-style fitting used the same 1,280 public plus all 85
ARROW labels, weights, preprocessing, architecture and seeds; only the 15 response
columns differ. External-cohort labels were not used for fitting, surrogate or feature
selection, thresholding or model choice in that comparison. The subsequent architecture
study reuses this already examined cohort and is therefore retrospective, even though
its final models do not fit these labels. The primary paired analysis and bootstrap
convention match the confirmatory analysis. Complete eligibility decisions,
source-exposure matches and molecule-level predictions are released as Supplementary
Data~5. Because the compilation is external and molecular identities are disjoint,
we call this an external molecule-disjoint cohort; we do not claim that all historical
measurement provenance is independent.

\subsection*{Additional molecular-model comparisons}

The graph and pretrained-representation comparisons followed a fixed protocol
specified before their fits. These are
retrospective extensions on the existing evaluation sets, not a second prospective
validation. The same public labels, ARROW folds and sample weights are used for every
model. Two direct predictors map molecular graphs to experimental hydration free
energy: a D-MPNN trained from random initialization and a CheMeleon-initialized
D-MPNN with its encoder fine-tuned.\cite{chemprop2019,chemeleon2025}
For each outer training set and seed, 15\% of each label source is held out internally
to select the epoch count by validation MAE, with an 80-epoch cap and patience 12.
The model is then initialized afresh from the same random or pretrained starting
weights and trained on the complete outer training set
for that count. Only this refitted model predicts the outer test set. Three seeds
(11, 29, 47) are averaged. Supplementary Fig.~6 illustrates the selection and refit
protocol; Supplementary Methods~S14 gives the complete configurations.

Two further comparisons use frozen pretrained representations: MoLFormer encodes
a SMILES sequence, and Uni-Mol encodes an RDKit-generated conformer.\cite{molformer2022,unimol2023}
Each is concatenated with the 2,265 structural features and fitted with the same
ExtraTrees ensemble as the matched control. We repeat the fit after adding the 15
response descriptors. This paired design tests whether the responses add value to
pretrained features without changing the downstream learner. The pretrained encoders
receive no hydration labels during these fits; their original pretraining membership
has not been audited for molecular disjointness. All model families and both members
of each paired comparison are reported, irrespective of their ranking.

These matched evaluations compare alternative molecular-learning approaches without
conflating different data splits or training resources.\cite{moleculenet2018}
We do not compare the reported errors directly with unfiltered FreeSolv leaderboard
values: 80 ARROW identities occur in FreeSolv, and the supervised response sources
require their own exclusions. A conventional FreeSolv benchmark would require
fold-specific refitting of every affected response source. The external 97-molecule
subset instead tests all six response sources with explicitly audited exclusions.

The computation-only source control was specified separately before its ARROW fits.
After that comparison, we extended the same ten-descriptor configuration to the
external cohort without retuning. Its OpenFF and GBn2 components were recovered from
the packaged surrogates and checked against the frozen corrected-response sums before
evaluation (Supplementary Methods~S17). This sequence distinguishes the retrospective
source analysis from the original prospective external evaluation.

\subsection*{Statistics}

MAE in kcal mol$^{-1}$ is primary. Root mean square error (RMSE), median absolute error and the fraction of
molecules improved are secondary. All comparisons are paired by molecule.
Ninety-five percent percentile intervals use 100,000 molecule bootstrap resamples
with seed 20260828. For repeated partitions, each molecule's absolute error is first
averaged across repeats before resampling. A predeclared comparison is positive when
the complete paired interval for candidate-minus-control MAE is below zero, negative
when above zero and neutral otherwise. We report all prespecified comparisons
without multiplicity adjustment and do not treat the panel as a single confirmatory
hypothesis test. No post-result feature selection is used within the confirmatory
comparisons. The later architecture search uses training-side validation and a
locked selection policy; its test intervals are descriptive, conditional on fitted
models and not adjusted for the many architectures compared.

\subsection*{Released ExtraTrees deployment artifact}

After evaluation was frozen, the response surrogates were fitted with standardized
benchmark equivalents removed and the endpoint was refitted on the 1,280 public plus
85 ARROW labels. This all-data artifact is used for deployment and the
endpoint-disjoint external evaluation; ARROW accuracy uses only the appropriate
fold-specific fits. Inference parses the supplied SMILES with RDKit and converts it to canonical
isomeric SMILES before computing descriptors and evaluating the six frozen
response-surrogate artifacts; three endpoint regressors are averaged. This step is
not a claim of invariance to tautomer, protonation or salt form. The runtime does not
read an experimental value, family label, scaffold, fold identifier, trajectory or
PIMD result. Software versions, hashes and a SMILES-only smoke test are reported in
Supplementary Methods~S12--S13 and Tables~7--8.
On the release host, warm CPU inference took a median of \WarmSingleSeconds{} s
for one molecule and \BatchSeconds{} s for a batch of 32
(\BatchPerMoleculeSeconds{} s per molecule). These measurements describe this
artifact and hardware; they are not a same-hardware comparison with physical
simulation or the other endpoint models.
