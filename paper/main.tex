\section*{Abstract}

Hydration free energy connects molecular structure to partitioning, binding and
reactivity. Physical calculations resolve the interactions underlying this quantity,
but their cost is incurred again for each new molecule. We ask whether their outputs,
together with empirical solvation measurements, can instead teach reusable molecular
features. Our model, SolvAI, learns 15 solvent-response descriptors from six external
sources and predicts them from molecular structure. A second model combines these
predictions with ordinary structural descriptors to estimate experimental hydration
free energy. Deployment requires only a molecular SMILES string and runs no physical
simulation. With an ExtraTrees endpoint, on the 85 neutral solutes of the ARROW reference set, the learned response descriptors reduce
mean absolute error from \MatchedMAE{} to \SolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ relative to an
otherwise identical structural-descriptor predictor. The gain disappears when the descriptors are shuffled
among molecules and persists when related chemistry is excluded from endpoint training. On
220 external molecules, error falls from \TierAStructureMAE{} to
\TierASolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$. Response augmentation also improves predictors
based on pretrained molecular representations. Relative to the full response set,
a computation-only variant performs better externally but worse on the reference set, showing that the useful source composition depends
on the evaluation chemistry. A broader comparison tests whether this representation
depends on a tree-based endpoint. A tabular foundation model reaches 0.199
kcal mol$^{-1}$ on ARROW-85 and, in a separate exploratory size-held-out analysis,
reduces error from 1.772 for the tree endpoint to 1.318 kcal mol$^{-1}$.
Neural endpoints also change the pronounced saturation of tree predictions on
capped peptide series. Together, these results show how reusable solvent-response
descriptors can support different prediction architectures while separating
small-molecule accuracy from transfer to larger structures.

\section*{Introduction}

Hydration free energy is the reversible work of transferring a molecule from the gas
phase into water. It connects molecular structure to partitioning, binding and
reactivity, and is a stringent test of how a model balances electrostatics,
polarization, dispersion, repulsion and solvent reorganization. Experimental values
are available for many small neutral compounds, notably through FreeSolv, but
predicting them with comparable precision remains difficult.\cite{freesolv2014,freesolv2017}

Physical simulation approaches the problem by resolving the solvent response. In an
alchemical calculation the solute--water interaction is varied along a coupling path,
and the resulting ensemble averages are integrated or combined statistically.\cite{mbar2008}
More detailed Hamiltonians improve the intermolecular interaction; path-integral
molecular dynamics (PIMD) can additionally represent nuclear delocalization, a
nuclear quantum effect (NQE).\cite{nqereview2018}
These ingredients can be accurate, but they demand repeated sampling for each new
molecule. The ARROW simulation study made this accuracy--cost relation unusually sharp: on 85
neutral solutes, classical ARROW has an exactly reconstructed mean absolute error
(MAE) of \ClassicalMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$, whereas eight-bead PIMD (PIMD8) reduces it to
\PIMDMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$.\cite{arrow2022}
Subsequent learned-potential work reproduced ARROW water-pair interactions and
quantum corrections within simulation, showing that parts of this description are
learnable while retaining trajectory-based inference.\cite{arrownn2023,nqe2024}

Data-driven solvation models offer a way to reuse information across molecules.
Direct predictors map a
graph, fingerprint or three-dimensional structure to a free energy, while
$\Delta$-learning and multi-fidelity models transfer information between calculated
and experimental targets.\cite{ramakrishnan2015,combisolv2021,rauer2020}
Graph models can also replace a particular implicit-solvent calculation by learning
its output directly from molecular structure.\cite{ward2021}
Physically inspired molecular descriptors can improve hydration prediction when they
encode electrostatics, polarizability, accessible surface and conformational
response.\cite{mldescriptors2023,fml2021} Other models retain physical computation at
prediction time: hybrid alchemical/machine-learning (ML) methods correct a simulated free energy,
ML-PCM learns from continuum-model components, 3D-RISM outputs have served as
hydration thermodynamic descriptors, and implicitly perturbed Hamiltonians represent
a molecule through its response to real or hypothetical solvents.\cite{hybridfepml2020,mlpcm2021,rism2020,imperham2022}
These studies show the value of solvent-conditioned representations; in these latter approaches, an
alchemical, continuum, integral-equation or ensemble calculation is still performed
for a new molecule.

A complementary line of work learns the physical descriptor itself. The ml-QM-GNN
architecture predicts quantum-chemical atom and bond descriptors before using them in
a reactivity model.\cite{mlqmgnn2022} Broader evaluations find that such descriptors
help most when they are relevant to the endpoint and can be recovered accurately,
and that a surrogate's hidden representation can sometimes be more useful than its
explicit predicted values.\cite{qmhelp2024,surrogatelatent2025} Most directly for
hydration, physically inspired solute descriptors have been predicted from molecular
graphs and passed to a second free-energy model, eliminating their per-query electronic
structure calculation.\cite{twostagehfe2026} These developments establish the major
architectural ingredients. Combining predictions from multiple chemical models is
also well established, as illustrated by the CATMoS consensus models for acute
toxicity.\cite{catmos2021} Our question concerns the information being transferred:
do complementary descriptions of solvent response remain useful for hydration
prediction when each is inferred from structure, and how far does their contribution
persist outside the chemistry used to fit the final predictor?

Our SolvAI framework learns 15 solvent-response descriptors from six sources and
combines their structure-predicted values with ordinary molecular descriptors
(Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}). We use \emph{response descriptor} throughout for one of
these 15 predicted quantities; the term does not imply a derivative with respect to
an applied field. The sources include calculated transfer free energies, empirical
solute properties and summaries of how water changes conformer energies. Their
predictions form a compact description of a molecule's interaction with solvent.

To isolate their contribution, we compare SolvAI with the same predictor architecture,
experimental labels, molecular representation, sample weights, folds and random
seeds after removing only the 15 response descriptors. We then compare direct graph
models and pretrained molecular representations, test chemical separation, and
evaluate 220 external molecules. The matched comparison identifies what the response
descriptors add to a specified learner; comparisons across architectures establish
how that learner performs among alternative ways to use the available data.
We then ask a distinct question: can a more adaptable endpoint retain useful
small-molecule accuracy while responding to molecular size beyond the training
range? This motivates a broader comparison of neural architectures and a tabular
foundation model, followed by size, homologous-series and uncertainty diagnostics.
The original controlled study and these subsequent retrospective comparisons
retain separate evaluation protocols.

\section*{SolvAI: learning solvent response before hydration free energy}

\subsection*{Two learning tasks with different supervision}

For a neutral molecule represented by a SMILES string $x$, the target is its
experimental gas-to-water transfer free energy $y=\Delta G_{\rm hyd}$ near 298 K.
We first train separate molecular models on external source datasets. Each model
predicts one or more source quantities, such as a continuum-solvent free energy or
a hydrogen-bonding parameter. These models are \emph{surrogates}: after training,
they approximate the source quantities without repeating the source calculation or
measurement. Their weights are frozen before learning the experimental hydration
predictor.

Writing the frozen surrogate predictions as $\widehat r_j(x)$, our model is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\boldsymbol r}(x)=(\widehat r_1(x),\ldots,\widehat r_{15}(x)),
 \qquad \widehat y(x)=h\!\left([\boldsymbol s(x),\widehat{\boldsymbol r}(x)]\right),
 \label{eq:solvai}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol s(x)$ contains a 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprint and 217 RDKit
two-dimensional descriptors. In the original implementation, $h$ averages three ExtraTrees
regressors trained on experimental hydration labels.\cite{extratrees2006}
It learns how the predicted source quantities relate to experiment, rather
than assuming that their sum is a physical free energy. All response descriptors are
predicted at deployment, including those whose source targets were experimental.
The response construction does not require a tree endpoint: our later comparisons
replace $h$, and in some cases the structural representation, while keeping the
15 source predictions fixed. We refer to the original implementation as SolvAI--ExtraTrees
when distinguishing it from these alternatives.

\subsection*{What the response descriptors represent}

The six sources describe different aspects of solvation
(Table~\ref{tab:sources}; Fig.~\ref{fig:learning}a).
Continuum-solvent calculations summarize the energetic response of a solvent model;
Abraham parameters describe empirical interaction tendencies; corrected explicit- and
implicit-solvent estimates combine a physical prediction with a learned experimental
residual. The conformational source summarizes both the redistribution of conformer
energies in water and the variation of solvation energy across conformers.
These are complementary targets, not successive levels on a single fidelity scale.
The source methods are described in the COSMOtherm, SoluteML, OpenFF Sage, GBn2, SMD and
ConfSolv literature.\cite{cosmo1995,combisolv2021,soluteml2022,openffsage2023,gbn22013,smd2009,confsolv2023}
The OpenFF 2.3.0 calculation records and the GBn2 dataset used here are identified
separately in Supplementary Methods~S3.
The Abraham descriptors quantify excess molar refraction ($E$),
dipolarity/polarizability ($S$), hydrogen-bond acidity and basicity ($A,B$), and
gas-to-hexadecane partitioning ($L$). Directed message-passing neural networks
(D-MPNNs) learn the two continuum-solvent targets; tree models learn the remaining
targets. Surrogate architectures and training details appear in Methods.

The target is a molecular equilibrium property, which includes conformational
averaging. A single molecule can have many conformers but does not have a separate
experimental equilibrium hydration free energy for each one. Our SMILES-based model
predicts that molecular property, not the hydration energy of a specified geometry
or a conformationally constrained state. The six ConfSolv descriptors provide
learned summaries of conformational effects; they do not reconstruct a molecule's
conformer ensemble at inference. This distinction also motivates our comparison
with Uni-Mol, a pretrained representation that uses a generated three-dimensional
conformer.\cite{unimol2023} Supplementary Fig.~7 illustrates the conformational
targets and their relationship to the molecular equilibrium property.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Separating source supervision from hydration evaluation}

We evaluate the predictor in two settings with different roles. ARROW-85 is a small
reference set with experimental and high-fidelity simulation values for the same
molecules. The external set contains 220 neutral solutes derived from original
Henry-law measurements, enabling a larger test outside the endpoint training set.
In both settings we compare predictions against experiment; the published ARROW
simulation results are a separate accuracy reference and never a SolvAI input.

The source datasets differ in scale and physical resolution. MolSolv
contributed 1,729,545 SMD(water) calculations, of which \MolSolvN{} structures
were used to train the confirmatory surrogate after quality and identity filtering.\cite{molsolv2022}
ConfSolv contributed 5,392,567 water conformer records; aggregation, target
availability and standardized-identity filtering left \ConfSolvN{} connectivities for
the response model. The archive counts refer to calculations for MolSolv and
conformer records for ConfSolv; the retained training rows are molecule-level
records. Complete definitions appear in
Supplementary Table~1 and source-specific provenance in Supplementary Fig.~2.

The experimental training pool contains 1,280 public hydration labels, supplemented
by the available ARROW training folds. Every response-source table excludes exact and
standardized equivalents of the 85 ARROW test identities. The 220 external molecules
are absent from all endpoint labels; 97 are also absent from every response-source
table. We report the full cohort and this nested subset because a known source response and a known hydration
label are distinct forms of supervision. The exact exclusions and the three affected
surrogate refits are documented in Methods, Supplementary Table~2 and Supplementary Data~4.

\subsection*{A matched test of physical information}

The matched, shuffled, chemical-separation and source-composition experiments below
use SolvAI--ExtraTrees. In five-fold out-of-fold (OOF) prediction, each ARROW molecule is evaluated by a
predictor that did not receive its experimental label. Removing only the predicted
responses from that predictor gives an MAE of
\MatchedMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}b). Adding the eight COSMOtherm,
Abraham and corrected-solvent descriptors lowers the error to \NarrowMAE{}; adding
SMD(water) gives \NarrowSMDMAE{}; and adding the six conformer descriptors reaches
\SolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$. The last value lies on the same accuracy scale as
ARROW/PIMD8 (\PIMDMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$), although the paired comparison does not
establish superiority over PIMD8.

At the molecule level, full SolvAI reduces the
absolute error for 53 of 85 solutes and reduces MAE by \SolvAIReduction{} kcal mol$^{-1}$
relative to the matched endpoint (95\% paired-bootstrap interval for SolvAI minus
the matched endpoint,
\SolvAICILow{} to \SolvAICIHigh{}; Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}c). The empirical and
residual-corrected subset also reduces MAE, with a paired interval entirely below
zero. The three computed-energy
descriptors (COSMOtherm and uncorrected OpenFF/GBn2), SMD alone and ConfSolv alone
yield smaller MAE reductions in isolation than the complete response set, whose
paired interval lies entirely below zero
(Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}d). The comparison measures the predictive benefit of combining
these descriptors. Molecule-level prediction,
residual and paired-change diagnostics are shown in Supplementary Fig.~1 and Supplementary Data~2.

Shuffling tests whether the benefit depends on assigning the responses to the correct
molecule (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}a). Across five independent
permutations of the 15-column block, the fixed-partition mean is \ShuffledMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$,
close to the 0.303 matched control and far from aligned SolvAI. The paired difference
between aligned and mean-shuffled descriptors is $-0.104$ kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% interval,
$-0.214$ to $-0.025$). Alignment between a molecule and its predicted response is
therefore necessary for the observed gain.

Partition choice changes the absolute point estimate but not the improvement
over the matched control. Across five complete five-fold partitions, matched structure-only
prediction gives \MatchedRepeatMean{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ on average, whereas SolvAI gives
\RepeatMean{} $\pm$ \RepeatSD{} kcal mol$^{-1}$; SolvAI is better in all five
partitions (Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}e; Supplementary Table~4). This places the observed error near the PIMD8
reference on this chemistry across partitions, without establishing superiority over
that simulation method.
Changing the prospectively frozen weight of available ARROW outer-training labels
from 3 to 1 leaves the contrast essentially unchanged: \WeightOneStructureMAE{} for
structure only and \WeightOneSolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ for SolvAI (paired change
\WeightOneDelta{} kcal mol$^{-1}$; 95\% interval, \WeightOneCILow{} to
\WeightOneCIHigh{}; Supplementary Table~9).

\subsection*{Comparison with direct and pretrained molecular models}

The matched structural control isolates a feature contribution, but a useful model
must also be compared with alternative molecular representations. Figure~\ref{fig:modern}
therefore adds a D-MPNN trained from random weights, a D-MPNN fine-tuned from
pretrained CheMeleon weights, and frozen molecular encodings from MoLFormer
(SMILES sequences) and Uni-Mol (three-dimensional conformers). For each frozen encoding we repeat the same
downstream fit with and without the 15 response descriptors. All models use the
same eligible experimental labels and test identities; model-specific training and
inner-validation procedures are given in Methods.

On ARROW-85, response augmentation lowers the MoLFormer-based predictor's MAE from
0.348 to 0.234 kcal mol$^{-1}$ and the Uni-Mol-based predictor's error from 0.334
to 0.220 kcal mol$^{-1}$. The advantage also persists on the external cohort and
its strict source-disjoint subset (Fig.~\ref{fig:modern}c). Each paired interval for
these two augmentation contrasts lies below zero. Thus, the response descriptors
remain useful alongside both sequence-based and three-dimensional pretrained
features, rather than helping only an unaugmented structural model.

The original SolvAI configuration has lower error than either directly trained graph
model on ARROW-85 and the 220-molecule external cohort. On the strict 97-molecule
subset, the difference from the scratch D-MPNN is unresolved: the paired MAE change
is $-0.023$ kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% interval, $-0.269$ to $0.231$). These cross-architecture
comparisons evaluate the specified training configurations; they do not
determine the best achievable performance of each architecture.

\subsection*{Transfer beyond nearby labels}

Random molecular folds can leave close chemical neighbors in the public training
pool. We therefore applied each separation rule to all endpoint-supervised molecules,
not only the 85 reference solutes. For each outer test partition, public and ARROW
training rows in the held-out family, scaffold or molecular cluster were removed.
A complementary analysis excluded every training row at or above a prescribed Morgan
similarity to any test molecule. The response surrogates remain fixed in these tests.

Response descriptors reduce MAE under all predeclared separation regimes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}; Supplementary Table~5).
At a train--test similarity exclusion threshold of 0.70, MAE falls from
\NNSeventyMatchedMAE{} to \NNSeventySolvAIMAE{} kcal
mol$^{-1}$; cluster separation lowers it from 0.349 to \ClusterMAE{}. Scaffold and
functional-family separation are much harder, but the same comparison favors SolvAI:
\ScaffoldMatchedMAE{} to \ScaffoldMAE{} and \FamilyMatchedMAE{} to \FamilyMAE{},
respectively. Every paired 95\%
bootstrap interval lies below zero. Response descriptors therefore retain value away from
nearby endpoint labels, even where absolute endpoint accuracy is not yet sufficient.
Molecule-level family errors and the four global separation regimes are expanded in
Supplementary Fig.~5 and Table~6.

We next removed all 85 ARROW experimental labels from endpoint training. Using only
the same 1,280 public training labels, the matched structure model gives
\ZeroArrowMatchedMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$
and SolvAI gives \ZeroArrowMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ (paired change $-0.128$; 95\%
interval, $-0.246$ to $-0.037$). This transfer test is not an independent external
benchmark -- the method was developed in the context of ARROW chemistry -- but it shows
that response descriptors retain value without fold-local adaptation to the reference set.

Finally, we evaluated a prospectively frozen external cohort derived from original
measurements in Sander's Henry-law compilation.\cite{sander2023} Eligibility was determined without
reference to model errors. Of 221 pre-existing candidates, one was excluded because an independent
registry audit had already established a conflict between the name, Chemical
Abstracts Service (CAS) registry number and structure. The remaining
\TierAN{} neutral solutes have no exact or standardized-equivalent identity in any
endpoint label used to fit the deployment models. On this endpoint-disjoint cohort,
MAE falls from \TierAStructureMAE{} to \TierASolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ (paired
change \TierADelta{}; 95\% interval, \TierACILow{} to \TierACIHigh{};
Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}d; Supplementary Table~10). Because some molecules occur in response-source
tables, we also froze a stricter subset of \TierAStrictN{} molecules absent from all
six response sources. The advantage remains: \TierAStrictStructureMAE{} to
\TierAStrictSolvAIMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$ (paired change \TierAStrictDelta{}; 95\%
interval, \TierAStrictCILow{} to \TierAStrictCIHigh{}). Thus, the response descriptors
remain beneficial beyond ARROW-85, although absolute errors are substantially larger
on the external set.

The external cohort also permits a direct view of error against distance from
endpoint-training chemistry (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}e). At nearest-training Morgan
similarity below 0.30, 35 molecules have MAEs of 4.027 kcal mol$^{-1}$ for structural
features and 2.501 for SolvAI. The corresponding errors are 1.227 and 1.014 for
103 molecules in $[0.30,0.50)$, and 0.822 and 0.724 for 74 molecules in
$[0.50,0.70)$. The eight molecules at similarity 0.70 or higher give an imprecise
contrast, with a paired interval spanning zero. Thus, the largest absolute
improvement occurs in the least similar bin, where substantial residual error also
remains.

The response set mixes computed quantities with empirical parameters and experimental
residual corrections. To distinguish their roles, an additional control (Fig.~\ref{fig:composition}) retains ten
computed-source descriptors: COSMOtherm, uncorrected OpenFF and GBn2, SMD(water), and
the six ConfSolv summaries. The predictor, experimental training labels and weights
remain unchanged. On ARROW-85 this control reaches \ComputedOnlyMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$;
its improvement over structural features alone has a paired interval spanning zero
(\ComputedOnlyLow{} to \ComputedOnlyHigh{} kcal mol$^{-1}$). The full 15-descriptor
model is better than this control by 0.032 kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% interval for full
minus computation-only, $-0.063$ to $-0.006$).
\par\endgroup

The ordering reverses outside the reference chemistry. The ten-descriptor model
reaches 1.095 kcal mol$^{-1}$ on the external 220 molecules, compared with 1.153 for
the full model. The paired full-minus-computation-only difference is $0.057$
kcal mol$^{-1}$ (95\% interval, $0.023$ to $0.093$). On the strict 97-molecule
subset, the corresponding errors are 1.422 and 1.536 kcal mol$^{-1}$, with a
paired interval of $0.058$ to $0.172$ for the difference. Computed-source descriptors
therefore reduce external error relative to structural features alone, while
the full source composition is more effective on ARROW. This comparison identifies
a dependence on source composition and evaluation chemistry, rather than a
single source set that is uniformly preferable. Both fixed configurations are
reported; external errors were not used to retune either model.

\subsection*{When response prediction is insufficient}

The choice of response target affects whether its predicted value improves hydration.
In exploratory development screens, compact ConfSolv response summaries transferred
better than graph or feed-forward latent representations trained on the same source.
Other candidate
signals -- including additional force-field diagnostics, pair-interaction data and
learned implicit-solvent hierarchies -- were non-improving in matched exploratory screens
(Supplementary Fig.~3). These screens test whether each representation can be inferred from structure accurately
enough, and with sufficiently non-redundant information, to improve the endpoint.

We also attempted to predict the mean derivative of interaction energy along an
alchemical coupling path, separately for the total, electrostatic and van der Waals
components. Source targets came from short two-bead path-integral simulations
(PIMD2) at coupling values $\lambda=0.1$, 0.5 and 0.9. Structure-only predictors
captured broad response trends, but
component MAEs ranged from 1.27 to 5.20 kcal mol$^{-1}$
(Supplementary Fig.~4). Adding the predicted PIMD2 response worsened the matched
exploratory endpoint from 0.196 to 0.201 kcal mol$^{-1}$; a predicted
classical--NQE--PIMD hierarchy reached 0.215, and their combination 0.219
in that screen. Direct integration of the predicted response curve, with fold-local affine calibration, yielded
1.51 kcal mol$^{-1}$ (Supplementary Fig.~4). No PIMD-derived coordinate is present in
the final model.

These exploratory results suggest three considerations when choosing response
targets: relevance to hydration, accuracy of their structure-based prediction, and
complementarity to the other features. They do not isolate a causal effect of
physical fidelity, because the source sizes, targets and learned representations
also differ. The exploratory analyses are reported separately in Supplementary Figs.~3--4 and Supplementary Data~1.

\section*{Discussion}

Our results establish a way to reuse heterogeneous solvation data in molecular
prediction. Separate surrogates learn the source quantities, and an experimentally
supervised predictor learns how their predicted values combine for hydration.
The matched and shuffled-descriptor controls show that molecule-aligned response
information improves prediction within the original ExtraTrees learner. Chemical separation and the external
cohort show where that benefit persists as the relationship to training chemistry
changes.

A descriptor set that is
best on reference chemistry can transfer less well than a smaller calculated-source
set. Adding empirical response information and residual corrections therefore has
to be assessed in the intended domain, even when the information improves an
in-domain benchmark. Our comparison establishes this reversal for the two evaluated
cohorts; choosing sources for a new deployment domain would require validation data
from that domain. The original 15-descriptor deployment model remains unchanged.

The broader endpoint comparison separates the representation from that initial
learner. TabPFN retains an ARROW error near 0.2 kcal mol$^{-1}$ and improves the
size-held-out diagnostic, whereas MoLFormer, graph and TabM endpoints demonstrate
different benefits from adding the response descriptors. No endpoint dominates
all evaluations. In particular, responses do not produce a resolved improvement
for TabPFN on the original external cohort, although they help in the size-held-out
analysis. This dependence on learner and evaluation chemistry is part of the result,
not a reason to select a different response set after seeing each test.

Architectural flexibility and physical transferability are different properties.
Removing the narrow tree-output range changes the peptide-series predictions, but
does not establish the correct free-energy increment per residue. Neither a neural
head nor in-context prediction supplies conformer-resolved energies, forces,
new-solvent dependence or exact size additivity without corresponding structure
and supervision. The size-held-out labels provide a more direct accuracy test than
unlabelled peptide curves, but remain an exploratory reuse of the development pool.

Predicted physical descriptors, $\Delta$-learning and computed-to-experimental
transfer are important precedents for this design.\cite{mlqmgnn2022,ramakrishnan2015,combisolv2021,twostagehfe2026}
The contribution here is their application to complementary solvent-response targets
with explicit tests of what those targets add. In these comparisons, the response
descriptors can be removed, shuffled, or added
to a different representation without changing the experimental target.

The transfer criteria are also consistent with systematic studies of quantum
descriptors, which emphasize endpoint relevance and accurate inference.\cite{qmhelp2024}
The present controls also test complementarity to the deployed molecular
representation. Recent work found that hidden surrogate
representations can outperform explicit predicted descriptors in some small-data
tasks.\cite{surrogatelatent2025} Here, by contrast, compact ConfSolv
response summaries outperformed graph and feed-forward latent coordinates from the
same source. The ordering is task-dependent: the useful representation depends on
which physical information survives structure prediction and remains complementary
to the endpoint features. The transferable signal is therefore not ``simulation'' in
the abstract, but an aligned, learnable representation of solvent response.

On the release host, the original ExtraTrees implementation's warm CPU median is
\WarmSingleSeconds{} s for one molecule and \BatchSeconds{} s for
a batch of 32 (\BatchPerMoleculeSeconds{} s per molecule). Its computational advantage
is the removal of per-query physical simulation, a property shared with the direct
molecular-learning baselines. The added role of SolvAI is to reuse source-response
supervision through explicit learned descriptors. Comparisons with the published
15-window PIMD8 workload concern total work on different hardware, not a same-hardware
kernel benchmark.

Validation currently covers neutral small molecules near room temperature, with
method development centered on the 85-solute reference set. The five-partition MAE
of SolvAI--ExtraTrees is 0.207 kcal mol$^{-1}$, increasing to 0.376 and 0.468 under scaffold and
functional-family separation. On the external cohort and strict subset, the full
ExtraTrees model retains a statistically supported reduction in error relative to structural
descriptors, but has larger errors
of 1.153 and 1.536 kcal mol$^{-1}$. These errors, and their dependence on chemical
similarity, motivate domain-specific validation before deployment. Per-query
reliability remains uncalibrated: the nominal 95\% TabPFN intervals cover only
80.0\% of the external cohort. Ions, salts, biomacromolecules and other solvents
remain unvalidated; the unlabelled size series do not expand that validated domain.

The unsuccessful alchemical-response experiment identifies a separate data need.
Predicting the interaction-energy derivative along a coupling path well enough to
integrate it requires more accurate component curves than the sparse PIMD2 sources
provided here. Additional protocol-matched response data would test that route;
adding more experimental endpoint labels alone would not supply those missing curves.

The broader opportunity is to treat costly computation as scientific supervision
with a lifetime beyond one query. Once a useful response is represented across enough
chemistry, its information can be predicted from structure and reused without
another solvent simulation. Hydration provides a demanding demonstration because the
accuracy reference includes explicit solvent and nuclear quantum effects. The same
principle may apply wherever physical computation exposes transferable response
descriptors that a deployed model can learn.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Reference set and targets}

The primary evaluation uses the 85 neutral organic solutes reported in the ARROW
hydration analysis at 298 K.\cite{arrow2022} One water condition was retained per
connectivity. The released benchmark table contains canonical structure,
experimental hydration free energy, experimental uncertainty where available,
classical ARROW, four-bead PIMD (PIMD4) and PIMD8 values, and all fold assignments. The exact
reconstructed PIMD8 MAE is \PIMDMAE{} kcal mol$^{-1}$; the source article reports
approximately 0.20 kcal mol$^{-1}$.

\subsection*{Endpoint labels and features}

The public training pool contains 1,280 neutral, benchmark-disjoint experimental
hydration labels: 1,147 connectivity-resolved records from a FreeSolv/CombiSolv-Exp
merge and 133 SoluteML dGsolvDB3 records supported by at least two source
measurements.\cite{freesolv2017,combisolv2021,soluteml2022} Each original ExtraTrees input has a
2,048-bit radius-2 Morgan fingerprint and 217 RDKit two-dimensional descriptors.
Non-finite values are handled by a median imputer fitted inside each endpoint fit.

\subsection*{Response surrogates}

Fifteen response descriptors are generated by six surrogate families (Supplementary
Table~1). CheMeleon-initialized D-MPNNs predict CombiSolv-QM/COSMOtherm and
MolSolv SMD(water) targets.\cite{chemprop2019,chemeleon2025} ExtraTrees models predict five Abraham
axes and the physical and residual components of corrected OpenFF and GBn2 responses.
LightGBM models predict six ConfSolv conformer and response-distribution summaries.
The latter are derived from the H$_2$O subset of ConfSolv. The final coordinate list,
target units, source sizes, transformations and inference calculations are given in
Supplementary Table~1. PIMD-derived targets are absent from this list.

The original endpoint is an ensemble of three \texttt{ExtraTreesRegressor} pipelines,\cite{extratrees2006} each
with 360 trees, maximum feature fraction 0.7, minimum leaf size 2, no bootstrap and
random seeds 11, 29 and 47. Predictions are averaged. Public endpoint rows have
sample weight 1 and available ARROW outer-training rows weight 3. The matched
structure-only model is identical except that the 15 response columns are removed.

\subsection*{Identity standardization}

RDKit 2026.03.5 was used to generate canonical isomeric SMILES, full InChIKeys and
connectivity blocks.\cite{rdkit2026} The confirmatory audit additionally applied
MolStandardize cleanup, largest-fragment selection, uncharging and canonical tautomer
enumeration. Exact and standardized benchmark equivalents were removed from every
supervised external source. The audit found no such match in the endpoint pool. It
identified 2 CombiSolv-QM, 32 MolSolv and 22 ConfSolv source records beyond the
original exact-connectivity exclusion. The affected surrogates were refitted with all
remaining molecules held in their original train, validation or test split. Full pair
lists, identifiers and split-verification hashes are released.

\subsection*{Confirmatory evaluation}

Confirmatory analyses followed a prespecified protocol archived in Git. Documented
implementation amendments preceded the corrected runs (Supplementary Methods~S9). The
primary partition is the existing five-fold assignment on the 85 molecules. Five
complete shuffled K-fold partitions use seeds 314159, 271828, 161803, 141421 and
173205. In each outer fold, all 1,280 public labels and the available ARROW
outer-training labels are fitted; the test target is absent. Feature blocks,
ExtraTrees hyperparameters, sample weights and model seeds are identical across all
comparisons.

A separately prespecified one-factor sensitivity repeated the fixed folds after changing
only the available ARROW outer-training weight from 3 to 1. It retained all source
rows, surrogates, features, hyperparameters and seeds. Its paired interval used the same
100,000-resample convention.

For the negative control, the 15 response columns are permuted as one block, thereby
preserving their joint distribution while destroying molecule alignment. Public,
outer-training and outer-test response rows are permuted separately within each fold.
Five permutation seeds (88001--88005) are evaluated for the fixed partition and each
of the five repeats.

\subsection*{Chemical separation}

Separation is applied to the complete endpoint-supervised pool. Functional families
are assigned by a deterministic SMARTS precedence; five outer folds are formed by
group. Bemis--Murcko scaffolds are canonicalized, with acyclic structures grouped by
primary family. Taylor--Butina clusters use radius-2, 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprints
at Tanimoto similarity 0.70. For each outer test fold, every public or ARROW training
row sharing a held-out group is removed. A separate nearest-neighbor analysis removes
every training row with similarity at or above 0.50, 0.60, 0.70 or 0.80 to any test
molecule. No threshold was selected by performance. Exact SMARTS, assignments,
training counts and maximum retained similarities are released in Supplementary
Data~3 and the accompanying \nolinkurl{results/confirmatory/} tables.

\subsection*{Zero-ARROW transfer}

The frozen endpoint configuration was fitted once to the 1,280 public experimental
labels without any ARROW experimental value. Both matched structure-only and full
response-descriptor models then predicted all 85 ARROW molecules. This analysis measures
transfer without reference-set endpoint adaptation; it is not described as an
independent external test because model development used the reference chemistry.

\subsection*{External validation}

The candidate cohort was frozen for a separate solvation-model validation study,
before that study's model predictions, using original-measurement records from
Sander's Henry-law compilation v5.0.0.\cite{sander2023}
The intrinsic Henry solubility constant $H_s^{cp}$ is the aqueous concentration
divided by the gas partial pressure. Values at 298.15 K were converted using
$K_c=H_s^{cp}RT$ and $\Delta G_{\rm hyd}=-RT\ln K_c$, corresponding to 1 M ideal
gas and 1 M ideal-dilute aqueous standard states. Here $K_c$ is the dimensionless
aqueous-to-gas concentration ratio, $R$ the gas constant and $T$ the temperature.
Qualification preceded SolvAI
evaluation and required one connected neutral solute, supported elements, compatible
target metadata and no exact, fragment-parent, uncharged-parent or canonical-tautomer
equivalent in the 1,280 public or 85 ARROW endpoint labels. One previously
documented registry conflict was excluded, leaving 220 molecules. A strict
97-molecule subset additionally excludes any identity-equivalent occurrence in all
six supervised response-source tables.

For the original matched ExtraTrees comparison, deployment-style fitting used the same 1,280 public plus all 85
ARROW labels, weights, preprocessing, architecture and seeds; only the 15 response
columns differ. External-cohort labels were not used for fitting, surrogate or feature
selection, thresholding or model choice in that comparison. The subsequent architecture
study reuses this already examined cohort and is therefore retrospective, even though
its final models do not fit these labels. The primary paired analysis and bootstrap
convention match the confirmatory analysis. Complete eligibility decisions,
source-exposure matches and molecule-level predictions are released as Supplementary
Data~5. Because the compilation is external and molecular identities are disjoint,
we call this an external molecule-disjoint cohort; we do not claim that all historical
measurement provenance is independent.

\subsection*{Additional molecular-model comparisons}

The graph and pretrained-representation comparisons followed a fixed protocol
specified before their fits. These are
retrospective extensions on the existing evaluation sets, not a second prospective
validation. The same public labels, ARROW folds and sample weights are used for every
model. Two direct predictors map molecular graphs to experimental hydration free
energy: a D-MPNN trained from random initialization and a CheMeleon-initialized
D-MPNN with its encoder fine-tuned.\cite{chemprop2019,chemeleon2025}
For each outer training set and seed, 15\% of each label source is held out internally
to select the epoch count by validation MAE, with an 80-epoch cap and patience 12.
The model is then initialized afresh from the same random or pretrained starting
weights and trained on the complete outer training set
for that count. Only this refitted model predicts the outer test set. Three seeds
(11, 29, 47) are averaged. Supplementary Fig.~6 illustrates the selection and refit
protocol; Supplementary Methods~S14 gives the complete configurations.

Two further comparisons use frozen pretrained representations: MoLFormer encodes
a SMILES sequence, and Uni-Mol encodes an RDKit-generated conformer.\cite{molformer2022,unimol2023}
Each is concatenated with the 2,265 structural features and fitted with the same
ExtraTrees ensemble as the matched control. We repeat the fit after adding the 15
response descriptors. This paired design tests whether the responses add value to
pretrained features without changing the downstream learner. The pretrained encoders
receive no hydration labels during these fits; their original pretraining membership
has not been audited for molecular disjointness. All model families and both members
of each paired comparison are reported, irrespective of their ranking.

These matched evaluations compare alternative molecular-learning approaches without
conflating different data splits or training resources.\cite{moleculenet2018}
We do not compare the reported errors directly with unfiltered FreeSolv leaderboard
values: 80 ARROW identities occur in FreeSolv, and the supervised response sources
require their own exclusions. A conventional FreeSolv benchmark would require
fold-specific refitting of every affected response source. The external 97-molecule
subset instead tests all six response sources with explicitly audited exclusions.

The computation-only source control was specified separately before its ARROW fits.
After that comparison, we extended the same ten-descriptor configuration to the
external cohort without retuning. Its OpenFF and GBn2 components were recovered from
the packaged surrogates and checked against the frozen corrected-response sums before
evaluation (Supplementary Methods~S17). This sequence distinguishes the retrospective
source analysis from the original prospective external evaluation.

\subsection*{Statistics}

MAE in kcal mol$^{-1}$ is primary. Root mean square error (RMSE), median absolute error and the fraction of
molecules improved are secondary. All comparisons are paired by molecule.
Ninety-five percent percentile intervals use 100,000 molecule bootstrap resamples
with seed 20260828. For repeated partitions, each molecule's absolute error is first
averaged across repeats before resampling. A predeclared comparison is positive when
the complete paired interval for candidate-minus-control MAE is below zero, negative
when above zero and neutral otherwise. We report all prespecified comparisons
without multiplicity adjustment and do not treat the panel as a single confirmatory
hypothesis test. No post-result feature selection is used within the confirmatory
comparisons. The later architecture search uses training-side validation and a
locked selection policy; its test intervals are descriptive, conditional on fitted
models and not adjusted for the many architectures compared.

\subsection*{Deployment artifact}

After evaluation was frozen, the response surrogates were fitted with standardized
benchmark equivalents removed and the endpoint was refitted on the 1,280 public plus
85 ARROW labels. This all-data artifact is used for deployment and the
endpoint-disjoint external evaluation; ARROW accuracy uses only the appropriate
fold-specific fits. Inference parses the supplied SMILES with RDKit and converts it to canonical
isomeric SMILES before computing descriptors and evaluating the six frozen
response-surrogate artifacts; three endpoint regressors are averaged. This step is
not a claim of invariance to tautomer, protonation or salt form. The runtime does not
read an experimental value, family label, scaffold, fold identifier, trajectory or
PIMD result. Software versions, hashes and a SMILES-only smoke test are reported in
Supplementary Methods~S12--S13 and Tables~7--8.
